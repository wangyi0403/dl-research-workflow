# Paragraph Function and Reader Flow

Use for a structural revision or when polished sentences still fail to establish the intended judgment. Keep diagnostic notes internal unless the user asks to see them or needs to decide an unresolved scientific question.

## Read before rewriting

Recover the paragraph's main message, support, necessary inference and relation to the neighboring paragraphs. A topic name such as “robustness” is not yet a message. A paragraph may contain connected subpoints; do not impose a one-sentence claim or uniform four-sentence pattern.

Reverse-outline only the affected section when needed. Identify duplicate functions, unexplained changes of object, evidence without interpretation, and conclusions with missing premises. Repair at the appropriate scientific or structural layer; a transition word cannot supply an absent relation.

## Guide the reader

Begin with enough familiar context to orient the new point. Put the entity whose action matters near its verb and keep pronoun referents clear. Place emphasis where the main new information can be recognized, often toward a clause or sentence ending. These are diagnostic principles, not fixed word-order rules.

Connect adjacent sentences through an actual relation: cause, contrast, evidence, consequence, refinement or qualification. Preserve the inference that changes the reader's understanding. Keep technical names stable rather than cycling elegant synonyms for one object.

## Verify the complete paragraph

After compression, confirm that the supported judgment, source references, material conditions and necessary bridge remain. Preserve warranted uncertainty and real alternatives; remove invented reader questions, empty process narration and repetitive apologies when they add no information. Read the handoff into the next paragraph. Return usable prose first; detailed before/after outlines are optional.
